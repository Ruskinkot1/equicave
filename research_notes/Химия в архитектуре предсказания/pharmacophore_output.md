## Q1. The classical methods (GRID, SuperStar, FTMap/FTSite, CCDC Hotspots API, AutoGrid, SiteMap)

### Takeaway
Spatially resolved, chemistry-typed interaction fields over a protein are a **40-year-old, well-trodden idea** — GRID (1985), SuperStar (1999/2001), FTMap (2009), Fragment Hotspot Maps (2016) all emit per-grid-point, per-probe-type scores, and all emit grids plus pseudo-atoms. The defensible gap is not "produce a typed field" but *how* the field is produced (knowledge-based propensity vs. forcefield vs. FFT-docked probes vs. learned) and, above all, **how it is quantitatively validated** — the classical validations are thin (21 fragments; 122 structures; case studies).

### Cited Findings

**SuperStar (Verdonk et al., CCDC) — knowledge-based propensity fields.**
- Computes *propensity maps*: an empirical, grid-based propensity for a given probe atom type, derived **entirely from experimental non-bonded contact distributions in small-molecule crystal structures (the IsoStar database, mined from the CSD)**. `[VERIFIED]` — [SuperStar: improved knowledge-based interaction fields for protein binding sites, J Mol Biol 2001, PMID 11273705](https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/11273705) (abstract read via Europe PMC API).
- Probes in the validated version: charged NH3+ nitrogen, carbonyl oxygen, hydroxyl oxygen, methyl carbon. `[VERIFIED]` same source.
- **Validation (this is the single most relevant precedent for "evaluate a typed map against the holo ligand's own atom types"):** propensity maps were generated for all binding sites of a **test set of 122 X-ray protein–ligand complexes**, and maps were **compared with the experimentally observed positions of ligand atoms of the matching type**. A peak-searching algorithm extracts hotspots. Mean distance from a ligand atom to the nearest same-type SuperStar peak = **1.0–1.2 Å** for the three H-bonding probes (**0.8–1.0 Å** for solvent-inaccessible ligand atoms), and **~1.5 Å** for the methyl-carbon probe (authors attribute the worse number to methyl interactions being much less directional). `[VERIFIED]` same source.
- Metal extension: propensity maps around Ca2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, validated on **24 metal-containing complexes**; coordination geometry derived correctly in ~75% of cases; with the derived geometry the mean ligand-atom-to-peak distance for an oxygen probe is **0.87(7) Å**, dropping to **0.59(7) Å** if the correct geometry is imposed. `[VERIFIED]` same source.
- Licence/runnability: SuperStar is **CCDC commercial** (it is the engine underneath Fragment Hotspot Maps and requires the CSD/IsoStar data). `[VERIFIED]` indirectly via the Hotspots API dependency statement below.

**Fragment Hotspot Maps (Radoux et al. 2016, CCDC) — the direct "pharmacophore map" prior art.**
- Method: "samples atomic hotspots with simple molecular probes to produce **fragment hotspot maps**. These maps specifically highlight fragment-binding sites **and their corresponding pharmacophores**." Intended use is explicitly medicinal-chemistry-facing: "an intuitive visual guide within the binding site, directing medicinal chemists where to grow the molecule and alerting them to suboptimal interactions within the original hit." `[VERIFIED]` — [Identifying Interactions that Determine Fragment Binding at Protein Hotspots, J Med Chem 2016, PMID 27043011, doi 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01980](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01980) (abstract read via Europe PMC API).
- **Validation (the headline number any new map would be compared against):** "validated using experimental binding positions of **21 fragments** and subsequent lead molecules. The ligands are found in high scoring areas of the fragment hotspot maps, with **fragment atoms having a median percentage rank of 97%**." `[VERIFIED]` same source.
- A thesis reports the same analysis split out as **98% median percentile rank for fragment atoms but only 72% for lead atoms**, which the author reads as the method distinguishing hotspots from the "warm spots" used later during elaboration. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [Cambridge repository thesis record, "The Automatic Detection of Small Molecule Binding Hotspots on Proteins"](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/82a99bca-7200-4679-9546-9f299d48081e) (quoted by search synthesis; I did not open the thesis).
- Beyond the 21-fragment test, validation is **case-study based**: protein kinase B and pantothenate synthetase examined in detail, with the maps "able to rationalize a Free-Wilson analysis of SAR data from a fragment-based drug design project." `[VERIFIED]` same abstract. **This is retrospective rationalisation, not a benchmark.**
- Contour-level semantics are part of the published interpretation: contour **≥17** reproduces natural ligand sites; contour **14** reveals "warm spots" where a fragment may not bind alone but can be stabilised when grown from an adjacent hotspot-bound fragment. `[VERIFIED]` — [Thomas et al., "Structure-guided fragment-based drug discovery at the synchrotron: screening binding sites and correlations with hotspot mapping", PMC6501894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6501894/).

**CCDC Hotspots API (Curran et al. 2020) — the runnable form of Fragment Hotspot Maps.**
- "Fragment Hotspot Maps builds upon interaction data mined from the CSD … Prior to this publication, Fragment Hotspot Maps was only publicly available through a web application." The API gives programmatic access to the core algorithm. `[VERIFIED]` — [Hotspots API, J Chem Inf Model 2020, PMID 32207937, doi 10.1021/acs.jcim.9b00996](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.9b00996) (abstract via Europe PMC API).
- **Licence (important and slightly deceptive):** "available from https://github.com/prcurran/hotspots **under the MIT license** and is **dependent upon the commercial CSD Python API**." `[VERIFIED]` same abstract, and confirmed on the repo: the package "is dependant on the CSD Python API - a commercial package"; you need a CCDC CSD licence (academic site licence typical); install requires CSDS 2020 activation with customer number + key. `[VERIFIED]` — [github.com/prcurran/hotspots](https://github.com/prcurran/hotspots).
- Additional dependency: **GHECOM** (open-source C program from PDBj), compiled separately, path set via `GHECOM_EXE`. `[VERIFIED]` same repo.
- **Output formats:** `HotspotWriter` writes an `out.zip` containing **`.grd` grid files** (described as large but highly compressible) **plus a PyMOL run script** for visualisation; `HotspotReader` reloads the zip; scored molecules/proteins are written via `MoleculeWriter` (e.g. mol2). `[VERIFIED]` same repo.
- Runtime: no timing figures published on the repo; SuperStar jobs are parallelised one-per-processor, **3 jobs by default, 5 when charged interactions are computed**, controlled by `nprocesses`. `[VERIFIED]` same repo.
- Demonstrated application: a workflow that **automatically derives protein hydrogen-bond constraints for molecular docking with GOLD** — i.e. the map is consumed by a downstream docking program, not just looked at. `[VERIFIED]` 2020 abstract.

**Ensemble / selectivity extension of hotspot maps (Hall & co-workers 2022).**
- "Ensemble maps" aggregate hotspot maps over an ensemble of structures of the same protein and then compare across related proteins to identify **selectivity-determining regions**. Applied to human bromodomain and kinase families; validation is "demonstrating that the method is able to identify selectivity-determining regions that have been used to introduce selectivity in past drug discovery campaigns" — again **retrospective case studies, not a metric**. `[VERIFIED]` — [Fragment Hotspot Mapping to Identify Selectivity-Determining Regions between Related Proteins, JCIM 2022, PMID 35020376, PMC8790751](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.1c00823).

**FTMap / FTSite / FTFlex / FTDyn / FTMove (Vajda & Kozakov, Boston University).**
- FTMap "samples **billions of positions of small organic molecules used as probes**, and scores the probe poses using a detailed energy expression. **Regions that bind clusters of multiple probe types identify the binding hot spots** in good agreement with experimental data." So the native output is **probe clusters (consensus sites), not a dense grid** — although the engine is FFT correlation on a grid. `[VERIFIED]` — [The FTMap family of web servers…, Nat Protoc 2015, PMID 25855957, doi 10.1038/nprot.2015.043](https://doi.org/10.1038/nprot.2015.043).
- Server family: **FTSite** (ligand-binding site prediction), **FTFlex** (side-chain flexibility), **FTMap/param** (user probes), **FTDyn** (ensembles). `[VERIFIED]` same source. **FTMove** adds ranking of binding sites across a structural ensemble. `[VERIFIED]` — [FTMove applied to nine SARS-CoV-2 targets, Viruses 2024, doi 10.3390/v16111647](https://doi.org/10.3390/v16111647).
- **Cost/runtime:** "By using **16 probe molecules**, the FTMap server finds the hot spots of an **average-size protein in <1 h**." FTFlex is proportionately longer because it maps all low-energy side-chain conformers. `[VERIFIED]` Nat Protoc 2015.
- **Self-reported comparative claim:** "FTMap is more accurate than classical mapping methods such as **GRID and MCSS**, and it is much faster than the more-recent approaches to protein mapping based on **mixed molecular dynamics**." `[VERIFIED]` same abstract. Note this is the authors' own claim in their own protocol paper; I did not find an independent head-to-head.
- Stated applications include **druggability assessment, identifying the ligand moieties most important for binding, finding the most bound-like conformation in unliganded ensembles, and providing input for fragment-based design**. `[VERIFIED]` same abstract.
- Input/availability: user submits a protein/DNA/RNA structure in **PDB format** to a **web server**. `[VERIFIED]` same abstract. The `sites.bu.edu/ftmap/` landing page returned **404** when fetched in this session (2026-10-08) — current hosting/licence terms unconfirmed. `[VERIFIED]` (the 404 itself).

### Inferences
- The classical field is **saturated on the "what" (typed grids) and weak on the "how well"**. SuperStar 2001 is the only classical method I found with a genuinely quantitative, population-scale, type-resolved validation (122 structures, per-probe mean distance to nearest same-type peak). Fragment Hotspot Maps — the method most similar in intent to a "per-sub-region pharmacophore map" — is validated on **21 fragments** plus two case studies. That is a low bar to beat and a clear opening.
- SuperStar's metric (mean distance from each holo ligand atom to the nearest same-type predicted peak, split by solvent accessibility) is a **ready-made, defensible, primary-source-backed evaluation protocol** that a deep model could be run through at modern scale (thousands of complexes instead of 122). Reusing it makes the comparison legible to the CCDC/CADD audience.
- The Hotspots API's MIT-but-needs-commercial-CSD structure means **Fragment Hotspot Maps is not reproducibly runnable by an unlicensed academic**. A genuinely open, weights-released equivalent is a real availability contribution independent of accuracy.

### Gaps
- **GRID (Goodford 1985, J Med Chem 28:849).** Europe PMC title/abstract search did not return it (searches returned unrelated 2026 docking papers). I therefore have **no primary-source confirmation** of: the exact energy terms (Lennard-Jones + electrostatic + H-bond), the probe (GRUB) library size, the validation in the original paper, the licence (Molecular Discovery Ltd, commercial), or the `.kont`/ASCII grid output format. All of this is `[UNVERIFIED]` and must not be asserted.
- **AutoGrid affinity maps.** Not verified in this session: the `.map` ASCII format, the per-atom-type map set, the licence (AutoDock4 GPL / Vina Apache-2.0), or runtime. `[UNVERIFIED]`
- **SiteMap (Halgren 2009, JCIM 49:377).** The primary paper did not surface in Europe PMC search. The Schrödinger product page I fetched confirms only qualitatively: it identifies "regions suited to hydrophobic groups" and "regions suited to ligand hydrogen-bond donors, acceptors, or metal-binding groups", distinguishes **sub-regions within a site** to assess ligand complementarity, ranks sites with **SiteScore**, and the maps are "meant to guide ligand modifications"; identified sites feed directly into **Glide** docking setup. `[VERIFIED]` — [schrodinger.com/platform/products/sitemap](https://www.schrodinger.com/platform/products/sitemap/). The page does **not** state output/export formats, does not mention **Dscore**, and gives **no licence or pricing** (demo/sales form only). So SiteMap's validation set size (I recall ~500 structures, `[UNVERIFIED]`) and its export formats are gaps.
- **SZMAP (OpenEye) and Cresset field points.** Europe PMC searching returned only unrelated solvation papers. No primary-source detail obtained on what they compute, how validated, licence, or formats. `[UNVERIFIED]` — treat as a gap.
- No per-protein **monetary** cost figures were obtainable for any commercial tool (all are quote-based).

## Q2. Deep-learning equivalents: models that output a per-voxel / per-point chemical-type field over a protein

### Takeaway
There are now **three or four published, code-released deep models in exactly this space** (PharmRL 2024, PharmacoNet 2024, PharmacoForge 2025, plus FMOPhore 2026 as a QM-based not-DL cousin). PharmRL in particular already produces a **dense multi-label field of six pharmacophore feature types at 0.5 Å over the binding site**. So a "per-sub-region pharmacophore map from a deep binding-site model" is **not novel as an output type as of 2026**; novelty must come from scale/architecture (e.g. equivariance, whole-protein rather than per-site-box), calibration, or evaluation rigour.

### Cited Findings

**PharmRL (Aggarwal & Koes, BMC Biology 2024) — the closest existing "dense typed field" model.**
- Output: a **"dense grid of feature confidences"** — the CNN is evaluated at **every grid point** of the binding site. **Six feature classes, predicted multi-label** so one point can carry several: H-bond acceptor, H-bond donor, hydrophobic, aromatic, negative ion, positive ion. Input is a voxelised protein box, so the output is a **field over the site**, not a per-atom prediction. `[VERIFIED]` — [PharmRL, BMC Biol 2024, PMC11687028, doi 10.1186/s12915-024-02096-5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11687028/).
- Geometry: **0.5 Å** grid resolution; box is a cube of **9.5 Å** edge centred on the query point; voxelisation via **libmolgrid** default atom types. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Training data: **PDBbind v2019** co-crystals, features extracted with **Pharmit**. **157,252 data points**, split ~104,835 train / 52,417 test; **3 CV folds with ligands at Tanimoto > 0.9 forced into the same fold** (i.e. they did ligand-similarity-aware splitting). **Adversarial negatives** added (grid points too close to protein atoms; predicted features too far from the complementary protein group). `[VERIFIED]` same.
- **Per-voxel classification performance: per-class ROC-AUC between ~0.951 and 0.982 (their Table 4).** Adding adversarial samples had "negligible impact" on classification performance. `[VERIFIED]` same. **This is the existing per-voxel AUROC number a new model must beat or contextualise.**
- Post-processing to discrete features: agglomerative clustering at **1.5 Å**, cluster centroids taken as features; **average of 136 features per DUD-E binding site**. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- A **deep geometric reinforcement-learning** stage then *selects* which predicted points form the final pharmacophore query — i.e. the authors themselves concluded that the dense field alone is not directly usable and needs a selection policy. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Code: "full open source code for training and inference" at **github.com/RishalAggarwal/Pharmrl**, plus a Google Colab notebook. **Pretrained weights are not explicitly stated to be released**; **no software licence is named in the paper** (the article is CC BY 4.0, which covers the article, not the code). `[VERIFIED]` same.

**PharmacoNet (Seo & Kim, Chem Sci 2024) — voxel intermediates, discrete pharmacophore output.**
- Architecture: **Feature Pyramid Network with a 3D Swin Transformer V2 encoder**; input **64×64×64 voxels at 0.5 Å** (32 Å box); atoms encoded by residue type, atom type and functional-group type; water and metals omitted. `[VERIFIED]` — [PharmacoNet, Chem Sci 2024, PMC11575537, doi 10.1039/d4sc04854g](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575537/).
- **Output structure (important distinction):** *intermediate* voxel-level outputs — **cavity masks for long-range and short-range interactions, per-token hotspot scores, and a density map per hotspot**; *final* output — **discrete pharmacophore points each with a centre, radius and feature type**, derived from the density map and a binary mask. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Types: six NCI types modelled (hydrophobic, H-bond, halogen bond, π–π stacking, cation–π, salt bridge); seven feature types used for scoring (hydrophobic, aromatic ring, anion, cation, halogen, HBA, HBD) with **fixed, hand-set weights 1.0 / 4.0 / 8.0** — the scoring function is **analytical with 7 weights and no learned parameters**. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Training data: **PDBbind v2020, 19,443 complexes**; 17,658 train / 1,500 val; **285 CASF-2016 complexes excluded**; 8 multi-ligand complexes dropped. Ground-truth pharmacophores derived from **PLIP**-identified interactions in the crystal structures. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Code/weights: GitHub **SeonghwanSeo/PharmacoNet**, GUI **SeonghwanSeo/OpenPharmaco**, **trained models and GUI on Zenodo**, MolVoxel on PyPI. The paper says "open-source" but **does not name a software licence**. Inputs: PDB, SDF, MOL2, or a fetched PDB ID; **visualisation via PyMOL**; **output file formats are not specified in the paper**. `[VERIFIED]` same.

**PharmacoForge (Flynn et al., Front Bioinform 2025) — generative pharmacophore placement.**
- A **variance-preserving equivariant diffusion model** that noises only the pharmacophore nodes on a protein–pharmacophore graph and uses **GVP-GNN** layers to predict noise (MSE loss). Generates **sets of 3-D centres with one of six feature types** (HBA, HBD, hydrophobic, aromatic, negative ion, positive ion), **conditioned on the pocket** (given by a reference ligand or a residue list). Number of centres is a **user input** (3–8 evaluated). `[VERIFIED]` — [PharmacoForge, Front Bioinform 2025, PMC12451294, doi 10.3389/fbinf.2025.1628800](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12451294/).
- Training: **CrossDocked2020** (>18,000 complexes, 22.5M docked poses); reference pharmacophores from **Pharmit**; center subsets of 3–8 randomly sampled as augmentation. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Authors' own stated limitations: **feature directionality is not predicted**, and **pharmacophore size is fixed rather than learned**. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Code: github.com/eflynn8/pharmacophore-diffusion + Colab notebook; **weights not explicitly mentioned; licence not stated**. `[VERIFIED]` same.

**FMOPhore (Nat Commun 2026) — QM-based, residue-resolved, not a voxel field, but markets itself as a pharmacophore map.**
- Computes a per-binding-site-residue **FP-score** classifying each residue as **Anchor / Transient / Accessible / non-hotspot**, combining **Percentage of Interaction (PI%)** and **FMO interaction energy (ΔE_FMO)**, with **PIEDA** decomposition into electrostatic, exchange-repulsion, dispersion, charge-transfer+mix, and solvation terms. Outputs include 2-D residue–ligand energy heatmaps, FP-score quadrant plots, and **"3D-FMOPhore" models showing favourable feature positions around hotspots**. A dynamic mode (Dy-FMOPhore) averages FP-scores over an MD ensemble. `[VERIFIED]` — [FMOPhore, Nat Commun 2026, PMC13328607, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72384-x](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13328607/).
- Apo mode scans with a **25-probe library docked with Glide SP** — i.e. it needs commercial Schrödinger. Also depends on **GAMESS (FMO2-DFTB3/PCM)**, Facio, PLIP, Maestro. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Licence: code on **github.com/DDU-CDD/fmophore** and Zenodo (zenodo.org/records/23080112) under **GPL and CC-BY 4.0** — but the workflow depends on non-open Schrödinger tools. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- **Output formats: the paper does not specify machine-readable export formats** (only figures/plots/3-D models). `[VERIFIED]` same.

### Inferences
- The "per-voxel chemical-type field over a protein" box is **already ticked** by PharmRL (dense, 6 types, 0.5 Å, ROC-AUC 0.95–0.98 per class). Any new contribution has to differentiate on one of: (a) **whole-protein / site-agnostic** field rather than a 9.5 Å box centred on a *given* query point — PharmRL's box requires you already know the site, which is a real limitation for a *binding-site model* that also wants to output chemistry; (b) **equivariance** (PharmRL and PharmacoNet are voxel-CNN/transformer, i.e. only approximately rotation-equivariant via augmentation; PharmacoForge is the only equivariant one and it is generative, not a field); (c) **calibration** (none of the three reports ECE or calibration of its per-voxel probabilities — PharmRL's paper explicitly does not claim calibrated probabilities); (d) **evaluation** (see Q3).
- A notable and quotable weakness across all three: **feature directionality is not predicted** (PharmacoForge says so explicitly; PharmacoNet's points are centre+radius+type; PharmRL's are clustered centroids). Direction/vector-valued features is an opening an equivariant model is naturally suited to.
- PharmRL's own design decision — train a dense classifier, then add an RL *selector* on top — is evidence from a primary source that **the dense field by itself was not usable for screening**; that is both a warning and a citation to lean on.

### Gaps
- Whether PharmRL and PharmacoForge actually ship **weights** (both papers decline to say) — would need to check the repos directly; not done in this session.
- No **software licence** is named in any of the three DL papers (PharmRL, PharmacoNet, PharmacoForge). The repos must be checked before claiming any of them is reusable.
- I found **no deep model that emits a field over an entire protein surface** (as opposed to a box around a supplied site centre or a supplied pocket). Searches for "interaction field prediction neural network protein grid probe" returned binding-site classifiers (metal-binding probe-based DL, Nat Commun 2026 doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74657-x; unified protein–small-molecule GNN binding-site prediction, PNAS 2026 doi 10.1073/pnas.2524913123) rather than typed-field predictors. This may be a genuine gap in the literature or a gap in my search; I flag it as **unresolved**, not as confirmed novelty.

## Q3. How such a map is evaluated — the crux

### Takeaway
**There is no community benchmark for pharmacophore-map accuracy.** What exists is a set of *ad hoc* protocols, which split cleanly into two families: (A) **geometric agreement with the holo ligand's own typed atoms** (SuperStar's mean distance-to-nearest-same-type-peak over 122 structures; Fragment Hotspot Maps' median percentile-rank of ligand atoms in the map, n=21; PharmRL's per-voxel ROC-AUC against Pharmit-derived labels); and (B) **downstream virtual-screening metrics** on LIT-PCBA / DUD-E / DEKOIS (EF, F1, BEDROC, AUROC). The DL papers have essentially abandoned (A) in favour of (B), which means **a rigorous, large-scale (A)-type evaluation is an available and defensible contribution**.

### Cited Findings

**Family A — against the holo ligand's typed atoms.**
- SuperStar: maps compared with "the experimentally observed positions of ligand atoms of these types"; metric = **mean distance from ligand atom to nearest same-type map peak**, reported **per probe type** and **split by solvent accessibility**; n = **122** complexes (+24 metal). Numbers in Q1. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMID 11273705](https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/11273705).
- Fragment Hotspot Maps: metric = **median percentage rank of ligand/fragment atoms within the map's score distribution**; **97% median for fragment atoms**, n = **21 fragments** plus subsequent leads. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMID 27043011](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01980). Thesis split: 98% fragments vs 72% leads `[SECOND-HAND]`.
- PharmRL: **per-voxel, per-class ROC-AUC 0.951–0.982** against **Pharmit-derived features from PDBbind holo co-crystals**, i.e. the labels *are* the holo ligand's pharmacophore features. Negatives are partly **adversarial/synthetic** (points clashing with protein atoms; features too far from a complementary group) rather than purely natural. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11687028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11687028/). **Caveat to carry into the report: a 0.95–0.98 AUROC against a label set whose negatives are hand-constructed is not evidence of chemical discrimination at the hard decision boundary.**
- PharmacoNet: labels = **PLIP-identified interactions in crystal structures**; the per-voxel masks and density maps are trained against these, but the **reported metrics are all downstream screening metrics**, not per-voxel accuracy. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11575537](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575537/).

**Family B — downstream screening metrics (what the 2024–2026 DL papers actually report).**
- PharmacoNet on **DEKOIS 2.0**: average **EF_α%, AUROC, BEDROC (α=80.5), PRAUC**; "outperformed AutoDock Vina and was close to Smina" (exact Glide/Smina values only in a figure). On **LIT-PCBA**: reported with **EF**, "outperformed Apo2ph4-Pharmit and PharmRL", and better than KarmaDock. Conformer ablation: **EF1% rises 3.951 → 4.459** going from 1 to 16 conformers. **DUD-E was explicitly discussed as biased and not used as a primary benchmark.** `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11575537](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575537/).
- PharmRL on **DUD-E diverse subset (8 targets)**: **F1 normalised by the best F1 achievable from the cognate ligand's own features** — i.e. the holo ligand pharmacophore is treated as the **ceiling/"ground truth"**, which the authors themselves note makes it an *enriched* reference. CNN-feature models beat the ligand-trained model on **5/8**; CP3A4 and CXCR4 exceeded the ligand-feature maximum; naive **top-3/4/5 CNN-ranked combinations gave F1 = 0 in all but one case (F1 = 0.028)** — strong evidence that an unselected dense field is not directly screenable. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- PharmRL on **LIT-PCBA (18 targets)**, screened in **Pharmit** with receptor exclusion: PharmRL_CNN better **F1 and EF** than **Apo2ph4** on **12/18**; **EF > 1 on all systems except ADRB2**. Metrics defined in the paper: **hit rate, precision, recall, F1, enrichment factor, Güner-Henry score** (emphasis on F1 and EF; hit-rate values not in main text). `[VERIFIED]` same.
- PharmacoForge on **LIT-PCBA (18 systems)**: best **F1 on 12**, best **EF on 13**, vs PharmRL (5 F1 / 4 EF) and Apo2ph4 (1/1); at least one query with **EF > 1 for every target**. On **DUD-E (102 targets)** vs reference (ligand-derived) pharmacophores: higher average **EF on 18**, max EF ≥ on **37**, higher average **F1 on 71**, higher **recall on 95** — **but 40% of generated queries returned no hits at all**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC12451294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12451294/).
- FMOPhore's validation metric is **recall only** — the fraction of holo-derived hotspots that an apo scan also detects. **Precision, false-positive rates and runtimes are not reported.** Reported counts are internally inconsistent (46 targets / 840 complexes in the intro vs 829 in the abstract vs "45 different systems" in the recall section). Most systems **recall > 0.8**; HIV, CB1, carbonic anhydrase II and KRAS–PDEδ are exceptions; JAK-2, PKB, PPARs and p38 reach **1.0**. Dy-FMOPhore tested on **7 systems with recall < 0.9**, all improved, some to 90–100%. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC13328607](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13328607/).

**Against FTMap's own probe clusters.** I found **no paper that uses FTMap probe clusters as the reference standard for scoring another method's map**. FTMap is instead used *alongside* fragment screens as a co-predictor (Q4). `[VERIFIED]` — absence across my searches; flagged as a gap, not a negative result.

**Against experimental hotspot data (SAR by NMR, fragment hit rates).** I found no systematic benchmark of this kind; only per-target case studies (Q4). `[VERIFIED]` — absence.

**Metric vocabulary actually in use, consolidated.** Per-voxel/per-class ROC-AUC (PharmRL); median percentile rank of ligand atoms within the map (Fragment Hotspot Maps); mean distance from ligand atom to nearest same-type peak, split by burial (SuperStar); recall of holo hotspots from apo input (FMOPhore); EF / EF1% / BEDROC / AUROC / PRAUC / F1 / recall / precision / hit rate / Güner-Henry (all DL pharmacophore papers, via Pharmit screening). **No paper I read reports calibration (ECE), per-type confusion, or a type-resolved top-k hit rate against fragment positions.** `[VERIFIED]` across the four papers read.

### Inferences
- The crux question has a clean answer: **no benchmark exists**, and the closest thing to a standard is *LIT-PCBA EF/F1 via Pharmit screening*, which is a **downstream proxy** and is shared by PharmRL, PharmacoNet and PharmacoForge — so it is the benchmark a new method **will** be measured against whether or not it is the right one. A new map paper should report LIT-PCBA EF/F1 through Pharmit against those three, or explain why not.
- The **genuinely open scientific gap** is a type-resolved, population-scale geometric evaluation: SuperStar's protocol at n≈10^3–10^4 with modern splits, plus per-type confusion and calibration, plus a top-k hit-rate against fragment positions. Nobody has published that. That is a defensible methodological contribution **in its own right**, arguably more defensible than the model.
- A serious hazard: every Family-A label set (Pharmit features, PLIP interactions, holo ligand atom types) is **derived from the single bound ligand**, so "accuracy" is bounded by the fact that one ligand does not probe the whole pocket. The authors of PharmRL say this out loud (ligand features are an "enriched" reference; CP3A4/CXCR4 *exceeded* the ligand-feature ceiling). This is exactly the argument for moving to fragment-screen ground truth (Q4).

### Gaps
- No per-voxel **calibration** or **per-type confusion matrix** reported anywhere I read.
- PharmacoNet's exact Glide/Smina comparison values are **in a figure only**, not extractable from the text I read.
- PharmRL's COVID Moonshot and LIT-PCBA raw numbers are largely **in supplementary tables** I did not open.
- Whether anyone has computed **per-voxel AUROC against fragment-screen-derived labels** (as opposed to single-ligand labels): I found no such paper, but cannot prove absence.

## Q4. Fragment-screening data as ground truth

### Takeaway
Crystallographic fragment screens are the right ground truth in principle and **have been compared to hotspot maps qualitatively, several times, never as a quantitative benchmark**. The published comparisons are sobering: agreement ranges from "all fragments sit in predicted hotspots" (PurC, classical difference-Fourier hits) to **2 of 16 fragment sites matching FTMap hotspots** (S. mansoni TGR). Dataset scale is large (XChem: >240,000 datasets, ~3,750 PDB depositions) but there is **no single curated, versioned, downloadable benchmark set**, which is itself an opportunity.

### Cited Findings

**The PurC study — the most explicit map-vs-screen comparison I read in full.**
- Hotspot tool: **Fragment Hotspot Maps (Radoux 2016)**; reproduces natural ligand sites at **contour ≥17**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC6501894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6501894/).
- In-house screen: **960 fragments** by DSF → **43 hits**, of which **8 confirmed by X-ray, all in the ATP adenine pocket**. **All fragments from this standard difference-map screen occupy positions the hotspot mapping predicted.** `[VERIFIED]` same.
- XChem/PanDDA screen: **Leeds 3D collection (125 fragments) + Diamond-SGC-iNEXT Poised Library (768)**; from **304 datasets, 88 PanDDA event maps**, fragments modelled in **35**. Eight binding sites enumerated (five front, three rear). `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Agreement: **only *some* PanDDA hits bind predicted hotspots; almost 60% of all hits occupy hotspot 1** (the ATP adenylyl pocket). **No quantitative agreement metric is given.** `[VERIFIED]` same.
- Discrepancies the authors explain away: **no fragments bound warm spot 2 or hotspot 3** (the CAIR phosphate site), so the authors suggest these "may not be true hotspots"; PanDDA hits with **occupancy ~0.4–1.0** "may not support elaboration to adequate affinity"; some hits form weak interactions in shallow pockets near crystallographic symmetry axes, attributed to PanDDA's sensitivity to low-occupancy binders; a negatively charged sub-pocket next to the ATP site was missed by earlier screening. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- The authors' reconciliation: low-occupancy PanDDA hits may be **"warm spots"**, useful if close to a hotspot-bound fragment. `[VERIFIED]` same.

**Counter-example with poor agreement.**
- *S. mansoni* thioredoxin glutathione reductase: FTMap found **11 probe-cluster regions**; of **16 experimentally observed fragment sites, only 2 (S3, S8) corresponded to hotspots**. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [Sci Rep 2024, doi 10.1038/s41598-024-52018-2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-52018-2) (reported via search synthesis quoting the paper; I did not open it).

**PPAT.**
- A 960-member fragment screen with X-ray follow-up identified **three ligand-binding hotspots** across the PPAT active site; "with a single exception, the main hot spot identified by **FTMap** binds the core compound found by fragment screening", and neighbouring secondary hot spots indicate where to extend. `[SECOND-HAND]` (search synthesis quoting the paper; primary not opened).

**Mpro / COVID Moonshot used as pharmacophore ground truth by a DL method.**
- PharmRL used **23 fragment-bound Mpro structures**; actives defined as IC50 < 5 µM; hit-to-lead set of **979 molecules (6% active)**, full set **2,062 molecules (38% active)**; results reported as raw F1 against **fragment-feature baselines (Fragment_Max and Fragment_Mean over 10,000 sampled pharmacophores)**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11687028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11687028/). **This is the one case I verified of a DL pharmacophore method using crystallographic fragment data as a reference — and it is a single target used as a baseline, not a benchmark.**

**Dataset scale and downloadability.**
- A 2025 review states XChem has run nearly **300 academic/grant-funded experiments**, collected **>240,000 datasets**, and completed industrial campaigns against **150 targets** (as of Aug 2024). `[SECOND-HAND]` — [Fearon et al., Applied Research 2025, doi 10.1002/appl.202400192](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/appl.202400192) (via search synthesis).
- A Diamond teaching deck gives **>300 academic projects, >240,000 datasets, ~30,000–50,000 liganded structures, 3,750 models deposited in the PDB**. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [CCP4/Diamond XChem course material 2024](https://www.ccp4.ac.uk/schools/DLS-2024/course_material/20241129%20-%20XChem%20-%20Daren%20Fearon%20and%20Blake%20Balcomb.pdf).
- SGC screens are **made fully public immediately and served on Fragalysis**. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [Diamond, Data Analysis and Collaboration Tools](https://www.diamond.ac.uk/Instruments/Mx/Fragment-Screening/Data-Analysis-and-Collaboration-Tools.html).
- A 2022 J Med Chem study on functionally diverse libraries uses a **10-target benchmark of 309 protein–fragment complexes from 225 unique fragments, 13–65 fragment-bound structures per target**, with structures on Fragalysis. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [PMC9421645](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9421645/). **This is the closest thing to a ready-made, citable fragment-position benchmark I found.**
- A 2023 JCIM fragment-merging paper lists four XChem test sets: **DPP11 (11 fragments), PARP14 (13), nsp13 (9), Mpro (19)**, downloadable from Fragalysis. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [doi 10.1021/acs.jcim.3c00276](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jcim.3c00276).
- A 2025 NSP13 helicase pharmacophore-screening paper used **51 XChem PanDDA coordinate files deposited in the RCSB PDB**. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [PMC12412281](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12412281).
- Programmatic access: an official **`xchem/fragalysis-api`** Python API exists. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [github.com/xchem/fragalysis-api](https://github.com/xchem/fragalysis-api). A **"Fragalysis Cloud"** FAIRness project exists under OSCARS. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [oscars-project.eu](https://oscars-project.eu/projects/implementing-fairness-structure-based-drug-design-through-fragalysis-cloud).
- A ~2026 OpenBind post mentions a release of **717 ligand-bound structures across three drug targets**, with FatA benchmark data and RdRp DENV-2/ZIKV binding events downloadable via Fragalysis. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [openbind.uk blog](https://openbind.uk/news/blog-openbind-0-advancing-open-molecular-structure-prediction/).
- Cautionary benchmark result: **SAMPL7 protein–ligand challenge** (community-wide, against fragment screening and pose prediction) reported pose-prediction success rates (<2 Å heavy-atom RMSD) of **0–25%** — though it tested docking/pose prediction, not hotspot maps. `[SECOND-HAND]` — [PMC9010448](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9010448/).

### Inferences
- **Nobody has turned fragment screens into a quantitative pharmacophore-map benchmark.** The ingredients are public (the 309-complex / 10-target set; Mpro's ~20+ fragment structures; Fragalysis + its API; PDB depositions). Constructing *"type-resolved top-k hit rate and per-voxel AUROC of a predicted typed field against PanDDA-observed fragment positions, by fragment functional group"* appears to be unclaimed and is **the most defensible single contribution** available here — it is a benchmark, which outlasts any one model.
- Two large confounds must be addressed up front or the benchmark will be attacked: (1) **PanDDA's low-occupancy sensitivity** produces hits in shallow/symmetry-adjacent sites that the PurC authors themselves doubt are real hotspots — so fragment positions are a noisy label and need an occupancy or B-factor filter; (2) **library composition bias** — the Poised/DSI libraries are not uniform over pharmacophore types, so a per-type hit rate must be normalised by the library's own type composition or it will just recover the library.
- The disagreement between the PurC result ("all fragments in predicted hotspots") and the TGR result ("2 of 16") strongly suggests **classical hotspot maps have poor recall on fragment sites outside the primary pocket**. A model that can be shown to recover secondary/allosteric fragment sites would have a real, measurable edge — and the metric to show it on is recall of fragment sites, not precision on the main pocket.

### Gaps
- **Live Fragalysis inventory**: the landing page I fetched (fragalysis.diamond.ac.uk) returned only the title "XChem @ Diamond" and no content; I could not obtain a current count of public targets or structures, nor the licence terms of the data. The published figures above are 2024–2025 snapshots at mismatched scopes (facility-wide vs public vs per-paper) and **do not sum to one number**. Must be checked directly before any count is printed in the report.
- **Licence of Fragalysis data** not verified (SGC data said to be "fully public immediately" — the actual licence string is unconfirmed).
- The PanDDA method paper (Pearce et al. 2017, Nat Commun) was not read in this session; the PanDDA event-map semantics used above come from the PurC paper's description.
- Whether the 309-complex 10-target set is distributed as a single downloadable archive (vs. assembled per-target from Fragalysis) is unconfirmed.

## Q5. Utility evidence: does a pharmacophore map improve a downstream task?

### Takeaway
**Yes, there is measured downstream evidence — but almost all of it is virtual-screening enrichment from the three DL pharmacophore papers, and it is measured against *other pharmacophore methods*, not against doing structure-based docking properly.** The strongest, cleanest utility claim in the whole literature is PharmacoNet's throughput argument (3,000–34,000× faster than docking at comparable-to-Smina enrichment), not an accuracy argument. Classical hotspot maps' downstream utility is evidenced by case studies and one concrete pipeline (hotspot-derived H-bond constraints for GOLD).

### Cited Findings
- **Screening enrichment vs other pharmacophore methods.** PharmacoForge: best F1 on 12/18 and best EF on 13/18 LIT-PCBA systems, vs PharmRL 5/4 and Apo2ph4 1/1. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC12451294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12451294/). PharmRL: better F1 and EF than Apo2ph4 on 12/18 LIT-PCBA systems; EF > 1 everywhere except ADRB2. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11687028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11687028/).
- **Enrichment vs docking.** PharmacoNet "outperformed AutoDock Vina and was close to Smina" on DEKOIS 2.0 (EF_α%, AUROC, BEDROC, PRAUC), and beat KarmaDock on LIT-PCBA. Out-of-distribution (unseen ligands): PharmacoNet **3.11** vs KarmaDock **3.16**, with KarmaDock falling **2.94 → 2.32** under the shift (the text does not clearly label the conditions). `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11575537](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575537/). **So: pharmacophore-map screening is roughly docking-equivalent in enrichment, not better.**
- **Throughput — the real measured advantage.** PharmacoNet: **0.39 ms/molecule** (1 conformer), **2.60 ms** (128 conformers) on a 32-core CPU; **~5.15 ms** for 70-heavy-atom molecules; **1.6M ChEMBL molecules in <10 min**; **187M ZINC20 molecules vs CB2 in ~20.1 h on one 32-core CPU** plus 43 min to rescore the top 1% against CB1; reported speedups **3,956× / 3,483×** vs AutoDock Vina (core/refined sets) and **34,117× / 27,731×** vs GLIDE SP; the Vina estimate for the 187M screen is **~11 years**. `[VERIFIED]` same.
- **Docking accuracy improvement from a map.** The Hotspots API paper's demonstration workflow "automatically derives protein hydrogen-bond constraints for molecular docking with **GOLD**" — a concrete map→docking coupling. `[VERIFIED]` — [doi 10.1021/acs.jcim.9b00996](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.9b00996). The abstract states the workflow but **does not report a quantitative docking-accuracy gain**.
- **Fragment growing.** Fragment Hotspot Maps' stated purpose is "directing medicinal chemists where to grow the molecule"; evidence offered is that the maps **rationalise a Free-Wilson analysis of SAR data** for PKB and pantothenate synthetase. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMID 27043011](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jmedchem.5b01980). **Retrospective rationalisation, not a prospective or controlled result.**
- FMOPhore offers **fragment-growing case studies** (Mpro, EPHA2, PKB, biotin carboxylase, BACE-1) plus a proteasome β5 mutagenesis comparison in which most experimentally identified resistance residues were predicted as Anchors, but **Gly-98 was missed and Gly-129 was a false positive**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC13328607](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13328607/).
- **Interpretability-as-utility, validated against humans.** A 2022 preprint trains ML on **>50 fragment-screening campaigns** including **experimentally validated misses as negatives** (which the authors say "matter significantly to model quality"), attributes each prediction to interactions between chemical environments, and **projects attributions onto predefined interaction prototypes (pharmacophores)** to derive fragment-binding rules. Validation: attributions compared against **substructure assignments made independently by expert molecular modellers**, with reported good agreement "even where the model's hit-versus-miss discrimination is far from perfect." `[VERIFIED]` — [arXiv:2204.06348](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06348). **Caveats: arXiv v1 only, no peer-reviewed venue shown, no numerical results on the abstract page, no code link, dataset sizes not given beyond ">50 campaigns".**

### Inferences
- The honest summary for the report: **a pharmacophore map buys throughput and interpretability, and roughly docking-parity enrichment — it does not buy accuracy over docking.** Anyone claiming a map is a contribution should lead with (a) speed at library scale, (b) a measured downstream coupling (constraints into docking, or fragment-growing vector selection), and (c) the benchmark gap in Q3/Q4 — not with "it's interpretable".
- The one **unclaimed** utility experiment that would be genuinely persuasive: show that a predicted typed field, used as docking constraints or as a pose-rescoring prior, improves **docking pose accuracy** (top-1 RMSD ≤ 2 Å) on a standard set. The Hotspots API paper set up exactly this pipeline with GOLD and did not report the gain. Nobody appears to have closed it.
- PharmacoForge's "**40% of generated queries returned no results**" and PharmRL's "**top-k CNN-ranked combinations gave F1 = 0**" are both from primary sources and both say the same thing: **the hard part is turning a field into a usable query**, not producing the field. A contribution that addresses query construction/selection has more headroom than one that improves the field.

### Gaps
- No quantitative docking-accuracy improvement attributable to a hotspot/pharmacophore map was found in any primary source.
- No prospective (as opposed to retrospective) fragment-growing success attributable to a map was found.
- PharmacoNet's "3.11 vs 3.16" OOD numbers are reported in the paper without clearly labelled conditions (per the full text I read) — do not over-interpret.

## Q6. What a platform user needs: presentation and interoperability

### Takeaway
The de facto accepted output contract is **(i) a set of scalar grids, one per probe/feature type, in a format a molecular viewer can contour, plus (ii) a viewer script, plus (iii) optionally pseudo-atoms / a pharmacophore query a screening tool can consume.** The only concrete, verified format set in the open-source world is CCDC Hotspots' **`.grd` grids + a PyMOL run script + mol2 for scored molecules**. Commercial product pages do **not** publish their export formats, which is itself a finding.

### Cited Findings
- **CCDC Hotspots API**: `HotspotWriter` → `out.zip` containing **`.grd` grid files + a PyMOL run script**; `HotspotReader` round-trips the zip; scored molecules/proteins via `MoleculeWriter` (e.g. **mol2**). `[VERIFIED]` — [github.com/prcurran/hotspots](https://github.com/prcurran/hotspots).
- **FTMap**: input is **PDB format** to a web server; output is **probe clusters / consensus sites** (hot spots identified by regions binding clusters of multiple probe types). `[VERIFIED]` — [doi 10.1038/nprot.2015.043](https://doi.org/10.1038/nprot.2015.043). The `sites.bu.edu/ftmap/` page 404'd in this session so I could not confirm current download formats. `[VERIFIED]` (the 404).
- **SiteMap (Schrödinger)**: presented to the chemist as sites ranked by **SiteScore**, locatable by size/functionality/solvent exposure, with **sub-regions within a site distinguished to assess ligand complementarity**, visualised in the platform, and feeding directly into **Glide** docking setup; maps are "meant to guide ligand modifications". **The product page states no export format and no licence/pricing** (demo/sales form only). `[VERIFIED]` — [schrodinger.com/platform/products/sitemap](https://www.schrodinger.com/platform/products/sitemap/).
- **PharmacoNet**: inputs **PDB / SDF / MOL2** or a fetched PDB ID; visualisation via **PyMOL**; ships a GUI (**OpenPharmaco**); the paper **does not specify output file formats**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11575537](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575537/).
- **PharmRL / PharmacoForge**: both emit pharmacophore queries that are screened in **Pharmit** (with receptor exclusion in PharmRL's LIT-PCBA protocol) — so **the Pharmit query is, de facto, the interchange format in the DL pharmacophore literature**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC11687028](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11687028/), [PMC12451294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12451294/).
- **FMOPhore**: outputs are **figures, heatmaps, quadrant plots and 3-D models**; **the paper does not specify machine-readable export formats**. `[VERIFIED]` — [PMC13328607](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13328607/).
- **CCDC Fragment Hotspots product page** — fetched, contained only the title and no substantive content; no licence, availability, format or runtime information obtainable. `[VERIFIED]` (the empty fetch) — [ccdc.cam.ac.uk/open-source-products/fragment-hotspots](https://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/open-source-products/fragment-hotspots/).

### Inferences
- A minimum viable interoperability contract for a new typed map, inferable from the above: **one scalar grid per feature type in a viewer-loadable format (CCP4/MRC or DX — both load in PyMOL, Chimera(X), Maestro and MOE), a PyMOL/ChimeraX script, pseudo-atoms in PDB for the discrete peaks, and a Pharmit-compatible query JSON for screening.** The `.grd` + PyMOL-script pattern of CCDC Hotspots is the proven precedent for the first two; the Pharmit query is the proven precedent for the last.
- **Caveat the report must keep:** that CCP4/DX load in PyMOL/Chimera/Maestro/MOE is my own domain knowledge, **not verified in this session**. The *verified* facts are only that CCDC writes `.grd` + a PyMOL script, and that the DL papers visualise in PyMOL and screen in Pharmit.
- Commercial vendors not publishing export formats means **"can a chemist get the map out of the tool"** is a real, unsatisfied user need and a legitimate differentiator for an open tool.

### Gaps
- `[UNVERIFIED]` — the exact grid formats of GRID (`.kont`), AutoGrid (`.map`), SiteMap exports, SZMAP outputs, and Cresset field-point files. None confirmed from primary sources in this session.
- `[UNVERIFIED]` — whether CCDC's `.grd` is directly loadable in PyMOL without the shipped script, and whether any of these tools emit CCP4/MRC/DX natively.
- No primary source found for how **Cresset** or **OpenEye SZMAP** present fields to a chemist; the OpenEye/Cresset searches returned unrelated solvation chemistry papers.
- No vendor publishes per-protein cost; all are quote-based.

## Bottom line for the objective (is a per-sub-region pharmacophore map a defensible contribution?)

### Takeaway
**The output type is well-trodden; the evaluation is not.** As of 2026, at least three code-released deep models (PharmRL, PharmacoNet, PharmacoForge) already emit typed pharmacophore fields/points over a pocket, one of them dense at 0.5 Å with per-class ROC-AUC 0.95–0.98. So "a deep model that outputs a pharmacophore map" is **not** by itself a contribution. What is genuinely unclaimed, and supported by the absence of any such paper in this search: **(1) a type-resolved, population-scale benchmark of map accuracy — SuperStar's 2001 protocol at modern scale, plus a top-k hit rate against PanDDA/XChem fragment positions normalised for library composition; (2) a whole-protein, site-agnostic typed field rather than a box around a supplied centre; (3) directionality of features, which every existing method explicitly omits; (4) a measured docking-accuracy or fragment-growing gain, which the Hotspots-API→GOLD pipeline set up and never quantified.** The benchmark a new method *will* be measured against, whether or not it is the right one, is **LIT-PCBA EF/F1 via Pharmit, head-to-head with PharmRL, PharmacoNet and Apo2ph4**.

### Inferences
- Framing advice for the paper: present the map as the **evaluation contribution plus a downstream coupling**, and treat the field itself as table stakes. Claiming the map *is* the contribution invites the response "PharmRL 2024 did this, with AUROC 0.97".
- The weakest link in all prior art is the **label**: every reference standard is one bound ligand's atoms (SuperStar, Fragment Hotspot Maps, Pharmit features, PLIP interactions). Moving the label to **many fragments on one protein** (XChem) is the scientifically correct move and is, as far as this search shows, unoccupied ground — but it needs the occupancy and library-composition controls noted in Q4.
